Event Security Planning: A Complete Guide for UK Organisers

Whether you are organising a music festival, corporate conference, private party or public sporting event, security is not an afterthought — it is a fundamental part of your duty of care. Getting event security right protects your attendees, your staff, your reputation and your legal standing. This guide covers everything UK event organisers need to know.

Start with a Risk Assessment

Every event security plan begins with a thorough risk assessment. This should be completed well in advance of the event date and reviewed regularly as plans develop. Key factors to assess include:

  • Expected attendance numbers and demographics — a youth music event carries different risks to a corporate dinner
  • Venue type and layout — indoor vs outdoor, single vs multiple entry points, capacity limits
  • Nature of the event — licensed alcohol sales, high-profile speakers, controversial content
  • Local crime statistics — historical incidents in the area
  • Time of day and duration — overnight events carry higher risk profiles
  • Previous incidents at similar events

SIA Licensing Requirements for Event Security

At events where alcohol is consumed and security personnel are used to manage admission, SIA-licensed Door Supervisors are legally required. Security Guards (a separate licence category) may be used for static guarding duties such as perimeter security or backstage access control, but cannot perform the door supervision role.

Failure to use properly licensed staff at a licensed event can result in the suspension or revocation of your premises licence. Always request confirmation of valid SIA licences for every officer before the event.

Calculating the Right Number of Security Staff

The appropriate ratio of security staff to attendees varies by event type, but general guidelines suggest:

  • Low-risk events (corporate, weddings): 1 officer per 100–150 attendees
  • Medium-risk events (public festivals, licensed bars): 1 officer per 75–100 attendees
  • High-risk events (large concerts, nightlife): 1 officer per 50–75 attendees

These are starting points, not fixed rules. Your risk assessment may indicate the need for additional resources at specific choke points, backstage areas or VIP zones.

Crowd Management Planning

Crowd management is a distinct discipline from access control. Key elements include:

  • Entry management — preventing dangerous crowding at entry points through timed entry, multiple gates and clear signage
  • Flow management — ensuring attendees can move freely between areas without dangerous compression
  • Emergency evacuation routes — clearly marked, stewarded and tested before the event
  • Capacity monitoring — real-time tracking of numbers in each area against safe capacity limits
  • Lost persons procedure — particularly important for family events

Search and Prohibited Items Policy

For events where there is a risk of attendees bringing in prohibited items (weapons, illegal substances, glass), a systematic search policy is essential. Consider:

  • Metal detection wands or walk-through arches at entry points
  • Physical searches by officers of the same gender as the attendee
  • Clear communication of prohibited items on tickets and signage
  • A secure confiscation and logging process

All searches must comply with the law and your officers must be trained in lawful search procedures. Noble Shield's door supervisors receive regular training in this area.

Communication and Command Structure

A clear command structure is essential at any event. Designate a Security Control Point staffed throughout the event, establish radio communication protocols, and ensure every officer knows exactly who their supervisor is and how to escalate an incident. Key contacts — venue manager, first aid team, local police liaison — should be accessible to security supervisors at all times.

Working with Local Police

For larger events, it is good practice (and sometimes a licensing condition) to brief your local police Licensing Officer in advance. They can advise on local intelligence, provide support during the event if required, and help ensure your event does not clash with other policing demands. Early engagement almost always makes events run more smoothly.

Post-Event Review

After every event, conduct a debrief with your security provider. Review all incident reports, identify what worked well, and note any areas for improvement. A good security company will provide you with a full post-event report and be willing to share observations that help you improve future events.
